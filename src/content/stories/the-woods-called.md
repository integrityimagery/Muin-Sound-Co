## The problem with outdoor ceremonies

Most wedding music is written for a room. It assumes a reverberant space, a
captive audience and a sound system — and then it goes outside and evaporates.
Wind takes the top end. Ground absorbs the bottom. Everyone is standing further
apart than they would be indoors, and the person playing has no monitor.

A song written for the woods is a different object. It sits in a narrower
range, it leans on instruments that project without help, and it leaves gaps
where the place can be heard. That last part is not a compromise. The birds and
the wind are why you chose the venue, and a song that talks over them is
fighting its own setting.

## What we ask about

The consult for a woodland ceremony spends more time on logistics than the
others, because the logistics are load-bearing:

- Where exactly you are standing, and what is behind you
- Whether anything is amplified, and if not, how far the furthest guest is
- What the ceremony actually contains — handfasting, vows, a reading, silence
- Who is walking, and how long the walk takes
- What the weather does there in that month, honestly

And then the part that matters most: what drew you to that specific place. Not
"we like nature" — the actual reason it was that clearing and not the next one.
That answer is usually where the song starts.

## Handfasting, specifically

<!-- PLACEHOLDER -->

A handfasting has a rhythm a song can follow: the approach, the binding, the
words, the release. That is a structure, and structures are useful — it means
the music can arrive somewhere rather than simply continuing until the
officiant stops.

Where the ceremony draws on a particular tradition, we will ask you what is and
is not appropriate to reference. We write from what you tell us about your own
practice; we do not borrow from traditions that are not yours, and we will say
so if a request is heading that way.
